Every speck of gold in Ohio rode here on a glacier, hundreds of miles south from Canada under ice a mile thick. Yes, you can still find gold in Ohio today, especially along the Clear Fork of the Mohican River near Bellville and Sandy Creek in the northeast, where glacial placer gold has been showing up in pans for well over a century.

With gold trading above $4,000 an ounce, you do not need to find much for a weekend of panning to pay for itself.

The mistake most first timers make is panning any river the glaciers crossed. The ice did not spread its gold evenly. It stalled against the Richland County hills and dumped some of its richest load in the Clear Fork valley around Bellville, and it left its most consistent gold in the Sandy Creek country to the northeast.

Get to those valleys and your odds change fast. Deadman’s Run outside Bellville still gives up color to patient panners, and the inside bends of Sandy Creek turn up flour gold and small flakes for anyone willing to wade.

The Biggest Gold Discovery in Ohio History

Ohio’s biggest gold story starts with a doctor who came home disappointed. He returned to Richland County from the California gold fields, and on a walk along a little stream near Bellville he noticed gravel that looked strangely familiar. He put a pan in the water, and it came up with color.

Word got out fast. Prospectors swarmed the valley of Deadman’s Run, a tiny tributary of the Clear Fork that locals started calling Gold Run. Assays reportedly showed the local gold was purer than Alaskan gold.

One landowner is said to have turned down $50,000 to lease his farm to speculators, a sum worth well over a million dollars today. With gold trading above $4,000 an ounce, you can see why he held on. A single ounce of Bellville color pays for a whole season of trips.

The glaciers spread that gold through every gravel bar in the valley instead of locking it into one vein, which made Bellville a panner’s prize rather than a mining company’s. The flakes never stopped coming. Panners still pull color out of that same valley today, and far fewer people are working it than in the rush years, so you will often have the water to yourself.

The Kinds of Gold You Can Find in Ohio

Ohio gold is glacial placer gold, ground fine by ice and water, and knowing what it looks like is the difference between a great day and an empty vial. The Ohio Division of Geological Survey says almost all gold recovered here is tiny flattened flakes about a millimeter across. All of it travels loose in creek gravel, within reach of your pan.

Flour Gold

Flour gold is gold ground finer than sugar, and it is the most common form of gold in Ohio by far. Glaciers are the best rock crushers nature ever built, so most Ohio gold arrived already pulverized. You will see it as bright golden dust in the crease of your pan after everything lighter washes away.

The lookalike is mica, which is everywhere in Ohio’s glacial till. One test settles it: shade your pan, because real gold stays golden in shadow while mica goes dull and floats. Flour gold slips out of a normal pan, so finish your concentrates in the Garrett Prospecting 10 Inch Backpacker Gold Pan. Keep the Ohio and Indiana Rocks and Minerals Field Guide beside you to check your gold against every mineral that imitates it.

Gold Flakes

Flakes are the next size up, flat pieces about the size of a grass seed that you can pick out and admire. In Ohio they turn up where creeks have had thousands of years to concentrate the glacial gold, especially in black sand layers on bedrock. A pan with two or three solid flakes is a great Ohio day.

The lookalike is chalcopyrite, a brassy copper mineral that breaks into small glittery pieces. One test settles it: poke it with a pin, because gold dents while chalcopyrite shatters. Flakes are nearly impossible to pick up with wet fingers, so the Garrett Gold Guzzler Bottle sucks them straight out of your pan.

Small Glacial Nuggets

Real nuggets do exist in Ohio, and they are the trophy find of Ohio prospecting. The state survey documents pieces up to wheat grain size, and lucky panners have turned up pea sized nuggets. Every one traveled hundreds of miles inside a glacier, which rounds the gold into smooth, worn shapes.

The lookalike is pyrite, and Ohio gravel carries plenty of it. One test settles it: look for crystal faces, because pyrite forms sharp little cubes while a true glacial nugget is rounded, dented, and butter colored. To stack your odds, the Garrett AT Gold Metal Detector is tuned for exactly this kind of small gold.

Knowing what the gold looks like is half the game. The other half is knowing where the creek hides it.

Where Gold Hides in Ohio Streams

Gold is about 19 times heavier than water, so it always sinks to the lowest, slowest spot the current allows. Learn these five features and you can walk up to any creek in this guide and know where to dig first.

Inside Bends

Water slows down on the inside of every curve, and slow water drops its gold. Look for the gravel bar that builds up on the inside of a bend and dig where the coarse gravel starts. In Ohio’s lazy glacial till creeks, inside bends are your most reliable producers. You will be moving packed gravel, and the Garrett 15 Inch Dual Riffle SUPERSLUICE Gold Pan is built for high volume panning.

Gravel Bars

Summer low water exposes gravel bars that were underwater all spring. The gold in a bar concentrates at its upstream end and down at the base, not on the surface. Dig test holes and pan the bottom material. Bars are full of oversized glacial cobbles, so screen them out with the SE Half Inch Sifting Screen and you double the gravel you process in a day.

Bedrock Cracks

Bedrock is the floor gold cannot sink through, so its cracks fill up like nature’s own sluice box. Where an Ohio creek cuts down to solid rock, every crack and joint has been collecting gold since the ice melted. The state survey recommends sampling gravel right at the bedrock contact. The bottom inch of a crack is the richest dirt in the creek, and the ASR Outdoor Steel Crevice Tool Set reaches what your fingers cannot.

Behind Boulders

Ohio’s creeks are dotted with glacial boulders, and every one splits the current to create a calm pocket directly downstream. Gold traveling in a flood drops right into that pocket. Feel around the downstream base of big rocks and dig the packed gravel your fingers find there. That gravel is usually cemented tight, so bring the Bully Tools 14 Gauge Round Point Shovel to break it loose.

Black Sand Streaks

Black sand is crushed magnetite and hematite, and it is the number one gold indicator in Ohio. The glaciers dropped it in the same load as the gold, and it settles in the same spots. When you see a dark streak in the gravel or a black layer in your pan, you are in the right place. At day’s end the SE Magnetic Separator Pick Up Tool pulls the black sand out and leaves your gold behind.

The Best Places To Find Gold in Ohio

Ohio’s gold clusters in four zones: the northeastern creeks, the Bellville gold rush country of the north central hills, the old glacial outwash of the southwest, and the streams along the glacial boundary in the south central counties. Here is every spot worth your gas money, grouped by region.

Northeastern Ohio

The creeks of northeastern Ohio drain thick glacial deposits, and bedrock lies close to the surface here, which gives the state’s most consistent documented gold somewhere to collect.

Sandy Creek (Carroll, Stark and Tuscarawas Counties)

Sandy Creek is the most talked about gold creek in Ohio, named by the state survey itself as a documented gold occurrence. It winds through Carroll, Stark, and Tuscarawas counties, and prospectors still work its gravel today. Your easiest access is at road crossings and on farms where you have permission.

The gold is fine flour gold with occasional small flakes, and careful panning is what works. Work the inside bends and pan down to the packed gravel.

Your pan will also show garnet sand, quartz pebbles, black sand, and the occasional banded agate the ice carried down from Lake Superior country. This is the moment people throw away good finds, because a wet unidentified stone looks like nothing. The Ohio and Indiana Rocks and Minerals Field Guide lets you check every odd stone against clear photos before it goes back in the creek.

Bring the Garrett 15 Inch Dual Riffle SUPERSLUICE Gold Pan and work the bends at low water.

Difficulty rating: 2 out of 5. Beginner friendly, wading optional.

Little Beaver Creek (Columbiana and Mahoning Counties)

The Middle Fork of Little Beaver Creek carries documented gold through Columbiana and Mahoning counties. It is a state wild and scenic river running through deep forested valleys with real exposed bedrock. Parts of it run through Beaver Creek State Park where collecting is not allowed, so work the stretches outside park lines with landowner permission.

Fine flakes in bedrock cracks are your find here, and crevicing is the technique. Work the rock joints and pockets wherever the creek runs over stone.

The gravel also carries pyrite, quartz, and black sand loaded with tiny garnets.

Bring the ASR Outdoor Steel Crevice Tool Set and scrape every crack to its bottom inch.

Difficulty rating: 3 out of 5. Rocky footing, wear real boots.

North Central Ohio

This is Bellville gold rush country, where the glaciers stalled against the hills of Richland and Ashland counties and dumped some of their richest load.

Clear Fork of the Mohican River (Richland and Ashland Counties)

The Clear Fork drains the entire Bellville gold district, which makes it the collector of everything the old rush left behind. It runs east through Richland and Ashland counties, gathering flakes from every gold bearing hollow. Long stretches are wadable, and road crossings give you easy access.

Flour gold with occasional flakes is your find, and standard panning works everywhere. Focus on the inside bends downstream of Bellville and pan the black sand streaks.

The pan also shows garnets, quartz, and shiny mica that fools every beginner once. Bellville still celebrates its Gold Rush Days festival.

Bring the Garrett Prospecting 10 Inch Backpacker Gold Pan to finish your black sand without losing the fine gold.

Difficulty rating: 2 out of 5. Easy wading, family friendly.

Before you toss that grey rock back into the Clear Fork, look twice. Half the stones in an Ohio pan look like nothing while wet, and some are agates, garnets, and flint worth keeping. The Ohio and Indiana Rocks and Minerals Field Guide has waterproof pages you can hold right over your pan, so you never guess again.

Deadman's Run and Wildcat Hollow (Richland County)

Deadman’s Run is the little stream that started it all, the site of the original Bellville rush, with Wildcat Hollow as its storied neighbor. Both are documented by the state survey, and both still give up color to patient panners. These are tiny waters in Jefferson Township south of Mansfield, more trickle than creek by late summer.

Fine flour gold in black sand is your target, and slow careful panning fits water this small. Pan the gravel right at the base of the little cut banks.

Your pan will show garnets, magnetite, and quartz. Both streams are private farmland, so a polite knock is your ticket in.

Bring the Garrett Gold Guzzler Bottle to lift every tiny flake out of your pan.

Difficulty rating: 4 out of 5. Small water, permission required.

Honey Creek (Seneca County)

Honey Creek is northwestern Ohio’s best documented gold water, a recorded native gold locality that flows into the Sandusky River near Tiffin. The creek cuts through deep glacial deposits, slowly concentrating the fine gold the ice left across this flat farm country. Nearly all the land is private farms, so get your permission first.

Flour gold is your reliable find, and gravel bar panning is the technique. Test the upstream ends of bars and any gravel sitting on the clay hardpan, which acts like false bedrock.

The gravel carries calcite, quartz, and plenty of black sand.

Bring the SE Half Inch Sifting Screen to screen off the cobbles and double the gravel you pan.

Difficulty rating: 3 out of 5. Flat easy walking, permission required.

Southwestern Ohio

Southwestern Ohio holds some of the state’s oldest glacial deposits, the state survey notes the earliest ice may have carried the richest gold, and Clermont County has more documented gold occurrences than any other corner of the state.

Stonelick Creek (Clermont County)

Stonelick Creek is southwestern Ohio’s flagship gold water, documented by the state survey and worked by local panners for generations. It flows southwest across Clermont County, shallow and slow with gravel bars around nearly every bend. Collecting inside Stonelick State Park is not allowed, so stick to private stretches where you have permission.

Flour gold with the occasional flake is your find, and gravel bar panning is the play. Dig the upstream ends of bars and pan the bottom material where it meets the clay.

Your pan will show quartz, flint chips, pyrite, and heavy black sand.

Bring the Bully Tools 14 Gauge Round Point Shovel and dig the bar heads down to the clay.

Difficulty rating: 2 out of 5. Shallow water, a great first trip.

Brushy Fork (Clermont County)

Brushy Fork is a small tributary of Stonelick Creek near Owensville, with its own listing in the state survey’s gold records. Small water like this concentrates gold fast, because every flood squeezes the whole flow over the same few gravel patches. It is more crawl than hike, and the banks are all private land, so ask first.

Fine gold in packed gravel is your target, and crevicing plus careful panning is the combination. Clean out the gravel pockets behind roots and boulders.

The gravel also holds garnets, magnetite, and the occasional agate.

Bring the Edward Tools Bend Proof Trowel to dig out every pocket, and treat this small stream gently.

Difficulty rating: 3 out of 5. Tight and brushy, permission required.

East Fork Little Miami River (Clermont County)

The East Fork of the Little Miami drains the same gold sprinkled Clermont County deposits as Stonelick Creek, with far more water doing the work. Prospectors report steady fine gold from its gravel bars, especially upstream of Harsha Lake near Williamsburg. East Fork State Park bans collecting, so work outside park boundaries with permission.

Flour gold in bar gravel and behind boulders is the pattern, and this is the best sluicing water in southwest Ohio where rules and landowner permission allow. Check the legal rules below before you run one.

Your pan turns up quartz, fossil coral fragments, and black sand.

Bring the Keene A52 Hand Sluice and feed it bar gravel from the upstream ends.

Difficulty rating: 2 out of 5. Big water, room to spread out.

South Central Ohio

The south central counties sit right on the glacial boundary, where the ice finally stalled, and the state survey documents color in two streams that cut the very edge of the gold bearing deposits.

Paint Creek (Ross County)

Paint Creek carries documented gold through Ross County toward the Scioto River near Chillicothe. It is a substantial stream that has been reworking glacial outwash for thousands of years, exactly the patient concentration that builds pannable gold. Bridge crossings and permitted farm access get you to the water, and Paint Creek State Park land is off limits to collecting.

Fine gold in inside bends and black sand streaks is your find, and panning is the technique. Low summer water exposes the best bars.

Your pan will show flint from the nearby ridges, quartz, and garnet sand.

Bring the SE 1/8 Inch Mesh Sifting Screen to classify those black sand streaks before you pan them.

Difficulty rating: 3 out of 5. Bigger water, watch the gauge.

Buckskin Creek (Ross County)

Buckskin Creek is Paint Creek’s gold bearing little sister, a documented occurrence flowing through western Ross County near South Salem. It drains the last gravels the glaciers dropped before the ice gave out. Access is entirely across private farms, so your permission comes first.

Flour gold in bedrock cracks and gravel pockets is your target, and crevicing plus panning is the combination. The creek runs low and clear by midsummer, which makes reading the gravel easy.

Your pan will hold flint chips, quartz, and heavy black sand.

Bring the ESTWING 22 oz Rock Pick to pry open the packed bedrock cracks and pan what you scrape loose.

Difficulty rating: 4 out of 5. Private access only, low water best.

More Proven Places To Find Gold in Ohio

The creeks above are where we would send a friend first, but the glaciers spread Ohio’s gold far wider than one shortlist. These next spots come from old survey notes, historical reports, and prospecting club experience, and every one earns your trip too. Rules and access change, so check current regulations and get permission before you visit.

Northeastern Ohio

Salt Creek (Wayne County). Old reports put fine glacial gold in Salt Creek, which winds through Amish farm country around Fredericksburg south of Wooster. The gold is flour fine, dropped by the same ice sheets that fed Sandy Creek. The banks are nearly all private farmland, so your polite knock comes before your pan.

North Central Ohio

Kokosing River (Knox County). Prospectors report fine gold in the Kokosing’s gravel bars around Mount Vernon, and the GPAA has held a member claim here reported as the Frazee Claim. The Kokosing is a state scenic river, so keep it to a pan and hand tools. Club membership or landowner permission gets you on the gravel.

Friends Creek (Richland County). Prospecting community sources list Friends Creek among the Richland County waters that still give up color, right in the heart of the old Bellville gold district. Look for flour gold in black sand pockets. The creek crosses private land, so ask before you wade in.

Swank Claim on the Clear Fork (Richland County). The Gold Prospectors Association of America has held a member claim on the Clear Fork near Butler, a separate stretch from the water covered earlier. Prospectors report flakes and plenty of garnet in the claim gravel. Access is for GPAA members only, so join before you go.

Central Ohio

Newark and Brownsville Area Creeks (Licking County). Native gold is documented from Licking County, and the old reports cluster around Newark and Brownsville where deep glacial outwash fills the Licking River valley. Pan the small feeder creeks where they cut into gravel banks. Collecting is banned in Blackhand Gorge State Nature Preserve, so stay on private stretches with permission.

Southwestern Ohio

Little Miami River (Warren County). Old accounts note gold across Warren County’s glacial deposits, and prospectors report fine color in the Little Miami’s gravel bars where the main river crosses that outwash. This is the main stem, a different water from the East Fork. Much of it is a designated state and national scenic river, so check current ODNR rules and get landowner permission before you move gravel.

South Central Ohio

Rocky Fork (Highland County). Rocky Fork runs east from Hillsboro through Rocky Fork Lake before joining Paint Creek, which puts it inside the survey’s Paint Creek tributary gold country. Try the gravel bars at low water. Rocky Fork State Park and the nearby nature sanctuary lands are off limits, so work only private stretches with permission.

North Fork Paint Creek (Ross County). The state survey credits Paint Creek and its tributaries with gold, and the North Fork drains those same glacial boundary gravels past Frankfort to meet Paint Creek near Chillicothe. Fine gold on the inside bends is your reliable find. The valley is private farm country, so ask before you wade in.

Spriggs Claim on the Scioto River (Scioto County). GPAA members have access to a claim reported near Lucasville, where the Scioto River reworks glacial outwash carried down from the gold bearing north. Prospectors report fine gold from careful panning here. The claim is open to GPAA members only, so membership is your ticket in.

The Best Gold Hunting Techniques in Ohio

Ohio’s fine glacial gold rewards patient technique over expensive machinery. These are the three methods that put Ohio gold in your vial, ordered from easiest to hardest.

Gold Panning (Difficulty: 1 out of 5)

Panning is the whole game in Ohio. Fill your pan with gravel, submerge it, and shake hard so the heavy gold sinks. Then tilt and swirl the light material off the top until only the heavy concentrate line remains.

With flour gold this fine, finish each pan slowly, because one careless swirl sends a day of gold back into the creek. Panning works everywhere in this guide, and it is the only method you need at Sandy Creek, the Clear Fork, and Stonelick Creek.

Crevicing (Difficulty: 2 out of 5)

Crevicing means cleaning out the cracks in exposed bedrock and panning what you scrape loose. It is slow and surgical, and it produces some of the best gold in Ohio because those cracks have trapped flakes since the glaciers melted.

Little Beaver Creek’s rocky gorge and Buckskin Creek’s low water stretches are the state’s crevicing headquarters. Work every crack down to its very bottom, because the last spoonful of packed dirt holds most of your gold.

A hand sluice is a small channel with riffles that you set in the current, then feed with gravel while the creek does the panning for you. It processes ten times what a pan can, which matters when the gold runs this fine. Check the legal rules first, because anything beyond a pan always needs the landowner’s specific okay.

In Ohio a sluice earns its keep on the bigger waters, the East Fork Little Miami, Paint Creek, and the lower Clear Fork, where gravel is deep and the flow is steady.

How To Tell Real Gold From Fool's Gold

Ohio’s glacial gravel is generous with sparkle, because pyrite, mica, and chalcopyrite ride the same currents as the gold. Two quick tests sort them out every time.

The shadow test takes one second. Shade your pan with your hand and watch the sparkle. Real gold stays rich and golden in shadow, while pyrite and mica go gray and lifeless the instant the direct light leaves them.

The poke test settles anything the shadow test misses. Press a pin firmly into the particle. Gold is soft metal, so it dents and holds the mark. Pyrite is brittle and shatters, and mica just slides apart in sheets.

The reason to get this right is simple. People who cannot tell the difference eventually toss a pan that had real gold in it, and you are not going to be one of them.

How Gold Got Into Ohio

Every spot in this guide sits where it sits because of ice. The glaciers did Ohio’s hard rock mining for you, grinding gold out of Canadian bedrock and hauling it to the state’s creeks.

The gold was born in ancient volcanic rock in Canada, in the same mineral rich belts that feed Ontario’s famous gold mines today. Starting about two million years ago, ice sheets up to a mile thick ground across that rock again and again. The ice plucked up the gold, crushed it fine, and dragged it hundreds of miles south.

When the last glaciers melted around 10,000 years ago, they dropped everything they carried across the western and northern two thirds of Ohio. Geologists call that dropped load glacial till, and the gold is scattered through it like pepper through a stew. Ever since, Ohio’s creeks have been washing the till and concentrating the heavy gold into the bends, bars, and bedrock cracks.

The glaciers stopped along a line that runs roughly from Canton down through Chillicothe to Cincinnati, and they dropped their golden cargo on the northern and western side of it. Stay on the glaciated side of the line and every creek you cross is worth a test pan.

The Best Time of Year To Hunt for Gold in Ohio

Timing your Ohio gold trip is about water levels, because the gold does not move but the creeks sure do. Here is your calendar, region by region.

Northeastern Ohio

Late summer through fall is prime time on Sandy Creek and Little Beaver Creek. Water drops to its lowest, exposing gravel bars and the bedrock runs that crevicers need. Your bonus window is right after spring high water recedes, when fresh gold has just been redeposited.

Skip the weeks after heavy rain. These creeks rise fast, run muddy, and hide everything you came to read.

North Central Ohio

June through October is your window for the Bellville country and Honey Creek. The little streams like Deadman’s Run shrink to a trickle by August, which concentrates your panning into the deeper pockets that remain. Farm access is also easier after crops are in.

March and April bring snowmelt floods. Let them pass, then hit the fresh gravel they leave behind.

Southwestern Ohio

Late spring through fall works in Clermont County, with September and October as your sweet spot. The creeks run low and clear, the gravel bars are exposed, and the humidity finally breaks. Winter panning is possible in mild spells since this is Ohio’s warmest corner.

After big storms, give Stonelick and Brushy Fork a week to clear. Small watersheds settle fast.

South Central Ohio

Late summer and early fall are best on Paint and Buckskin creeks. Low water exposes the bedrock stretches that hold the region’s best gold, and the farm access roads are dry. Crisp October days here are some of the best panning weather in the state.

Spring floods on Paint Creek are serious. Wait for the gauge to drop before you wade.

Legal Rules for Gold Prospecting in Ohio

We are rockhounds, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. Rules change, and staying legal is your responsibility.

Private Property

Nearly every gold bearing creek in Ohio flows through private land, and the adjoining landowner generally owns the streambed too. Wading up a creek without permission is trespassing even if you entered at a public bridge. Written permission is the most important tool you can carry, and rural landowners often say yes to a polite ask.

Protected and Restricted Land

Ohio state parks and state nature preserves prohibit collecting, which rules out the park sections of Little Beaver, Stonelick, East Fork, and Paint creeks. The bright spot is the Ohio Division of Geological Survey, which confirms that hobby panning needs no state permit as long as it stays recreational, recovers negligible commercial value, and does no environmental damage. Ohio’s state forests list gold panning among their permitted activities, which makes Mohican Memorial State Forest country worth your look.

Active Mining Claims

The Gold Prospectors Association of America has held member claims in Ohio, including stretches of the Kokosing, the Clear Fork, and the Scioto covered above. Those claims are open to GPAA members only, so join before you go.

Equipment Restrictions

Hand pans and hand tools are the safe baseline everywhere panning is legal. Suction dredges and other motorized equipment fall under federal Clean Water Act permitting through the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and Ohio EPA. Keep it to a pan, a shovel, and hand tools and you stay inside the hobby exemption.

Play it safe on every trip. Confirm access with the landowner or the managing agency before you dig, because boundaries and rules change without notice.

When in doubt, treat a spot as look but do not take until you have confirmation in hand.

The best shortcut is local knowledge. Ohio’s gold prospecting clubs know which landowners welcome panners, and a membership pays for itself in access alone.

Ohio Gold FAQ

Is there gold in Ohio?

Yes, there is gold in Ohio, all of it glacial placer gold carried down from Canada by ice age glaciers. It shows up as flour gold and small flakes in creeks across the glaciated two thirds of the state, all of it in stream gravel within reach of your pan.

Where is the best place to find gold in Ohio?

Sandy Creek in northeastern Ohio and the Clear Fork of the Mohican River near Bellville are the most proven producers, along with Stonelick Creek in Clermont County. All three carry documented gold and have been panned successfully for generations. The best places section above breaks down access for every spot.

Is it legal to pan for gold in Ohio?

Recreational gold panning is legal in Ohio and needs no state permit, according to the Ohio Department of Natural Resources, as long as it stays a hobby with hand tools. You must have landowner permission on private land, and collecting is banned in state parks and nature preserves.

Has anyone ever found a gold nugget in Ohio?

Yes, the state survey documents small glacial nuggets up to pea size, each one carried down from Canada and rounded smooth by the ice. The viral story about a 40 pound Ohio nugget is a social media hoax, so trust the real record. A true nugget is the trophy every Ohio panner works toward.

What creeks in Ohio have gold?

The state’s documented gold creeks include Sandy Creek, the Middle Fork of Little Beaver Creek, the Clear Fork of the Mohican River, Deadman’s Run, Honey Creek, Stonelick Creek, Brushy Fork, Paint Creek, and Buckskin Creek. Most creeks in glaciated Ohio can show you a trace of color, and the proven producers above are where your odds are best.
